Septic Vs. Sewer: What Palm Beach County Homeowners Need To Know

Written by the West Palm Beach 24/7 Plumbing team

Most homes in the more developed parts of this area connect to municipal sewer service, but unincorporated pockets of Palm Beach County, along with some older neighborhoods that were never annexed, still rely entirely on septic systems. If you're not sure which category your property falls into, it's worth finding out before a problem forces the question.

How To Tell Which System You Have

If you pay a sewer charge on your water utility bill, you're on municipal sewer. If you don't, and especially if your property is in a more rural or unincorporated part of the county, you're very likely on septic. A quick look at your property's utility records or a call to the county can confirm it definitively if you're unsure.

What A Septic System Actually Does

A septic system treats wastewater on-site rather than sending it to a municipal treatment facility. Solid waste settles in a tank, where bacteria break it down, while liquid effluent flows out into a drain field that filters it through the soil. It's a self-contained system, which means its maintenance is entirely the homeowner's responsibility.

Why Regular Pumping Matters

Most residential septic tanks need pumping every three to five years, depending on household size and usage. Skipping this isn't a minor oversight, an overdue tank risks backing up into the house or, worse, damaging the drain field, which is a far more expensive repair than routine pumping ever would have been.

Warning Signs Something's Wrong

Slow drains throughout the house (not just one fixture), a sewage odor near the yard, and standing water or unusually lush grass over the drain field are all signs the system needs attention, either overdue pumping or, in more serious cases, drain field repair.

The Region's Water Table Adds A Wrinkle

This area's naturally high water table can affect drain field performance more than it would in drier soil conditions elsewhere, which is part of why septic systems here sometimes need more attentive monitoring than the standard maintenance interval alone would suggest.

What Not To Put Down The Drains On A Septic System

Grease, harsh chemicals, and non-biodegradable items are harder on a septic system than on municipal sewer, since there's no large-scale treatment facility processing what goes down your drains, just the bacteria in your own tank. Being mindful of this meaningfully extends how long the system performs well between pumpings.

Buying Or Selling A Home On Septic

A septic inspection is worth including as part of any real estate transaction involving a septic property, the same way a sewer camera inspection matters for a home on municipal sewer. The tank's condition and pumping history aren't always obvious from a standard home inspection alone.

Signs A Drain Field May Be Failing Rather Than Just A Full Tank

A full tank and a failing drain field can look similar at first (slow drains, odors) but they're different problems with different fixes. Pumping resolves a full tank quickly. A failing drain field, often caused by soil compaction, root intrusion, or age, may need a more involved repair or, in serious cases, partial replacement. An inspection that checks both distinguishes between the two before you pay for a pumping service that won't actually solve a drain field problem.

The Bottom Line

Whether your property is on septic or municipal sewer isn't something most homeowners think about until a problem forces the question, but knowing which system you have, and staying ahead of its specific maintenance needs, is the difference between a routine service call and an expensive emergency. Septic systems in particular reward proactive maintenance precisely because they're entirely the homeowner's responsibility with no municipal backstop, and this region's high water table makes staying ahead of drain field issues especially worthwhile rather than waiting for obvious symptoms to appear. If you've recently moved into a property and aren't certain which system it has, that's worth confirming in your first few weeks rather than waiting for a slow drain to raise the question, since the answer changes both your maintenance responsibilities and who you'd call for a future issue.

Quick Recap

Know whether you're on septic or sewer, keep pumping on a three-to-five-year schedule if you're on septic, and treat slow drains throughout the house or yard odors as a reason to schedule an inspection rather than wait it out.

Frequently Asked

Can a septic system be connected to municipal sewer later if it becomes available?

In some cases, yes, though it typically requires a permitted connection project and isn't something most homeowners take on without a specific reason to switch.

Does a septic inspection check both the tank and the drain field?

A thorough inspection should check both. A tank that's simply full looks different, and requires a different fix, than a drain field that's actually failing.

How do I find out when my septic tank was last pumped?

If you don't have records from a previous owner or service provider, we can assess the tank's current condition during an inspection, which gives a reasonable indication of whether it's overdue, and we can also help you start a simple maintenance log going forward so this isn't a mystery again in a few years.

Is there anything special I should do before a septic pumping appointment?

Generally no special preparation is needed, though making sure the tank's access lid or cleanout is reachable, not blocked by landscaping or parked vehicles, helps the appointment go faster.
